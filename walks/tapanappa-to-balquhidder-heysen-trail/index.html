Overview

The Tapanappa to Balquhidder section of the Heysen Trail South Coast is, in a word, wild. It covers 14.75 km of some of the most remote and spectacular coastal terrain in South Australia — clifftop single track, a kilometre-and-a-half of deserted wilderness beach and serious headland scrambles — with 599 m of ascent packed into a walk that demands respect and preparation.

This section sits between Delamere and Balquhidder on the 73 km Heysen Trail South Coast route — a multi-day track running from Cape Jervis to Victor Harbor. As a standalone day walk, it’s classified Hard — not because the terrain is technically extreme, but because of the commitment level involved: remote location, no water, no phone coverage, tide-dependent beach crossing and genuinely dangerous cliff edges.

Get it right and it’s one of the finest day walks in South Australia.

This is a Remote and Committing Walk

No roads access the route between Tapanappa and Balquhidder. No phone coverage for most of the section. No water on route. The tidal beach section can close at high tide. Share your trip plan with someone before departing, carry a PLB or satellite communicator, and do not attempt this walk in poor weather.

The Route in Detail

Tapanappa Lookout to Boat Harbour (km 0 – 2.5)

The walk begins at Tapanappa Lookout — a broad clifftop viewpoint above 200 m that on a clear morning delivers views west toward Cape Jervis (and the lighthouse there), east along the wild southern Fleurieu coast and south to the endless Southern Ocean. Start early to catch it in morning light.

The trail drops immediately eastward from the lookout on narrow single track along the cliff edge. This is technically straightforward but psychologically intense: the track sits close to unfenced drops of 50–100 m onto coastal rocks below. Stay well back from the edge and keep dogs leashed if you have them (though the Conservation Park sections don’t permit dogs).

Boat Harbour appears around km 2.5 — a small, rocky cove carved into the base of the cliffs. The cove can be reached from the trail with care, though access to the water is difficult. More valuably, it’s one of the most reliable dolphin viewing spots on the Fleurieu coast.

Boat Harbour — Dolphin and Seal Viewing

Bottlenose dolphins regularly work the kelp beds in and around Boat Harbour, visible from the cliff track above. New Zealand fur seals haul out on the rocky outcrops at the base of the cove. Between June and October, southern right whales migrate along this stretch of coast — the clifftop vantage is exceptional.

The Descent to Tunkalilla Beach (km 2.5 – 5.5)

After Boat Harbour the trail climbs briefly before beginning the long descent toward Tunkalilla Beach. This is the most technically demanding descent of the walk — steep, rocky, loose in sections and unforgiving if the ground is wet.

The descent takes approximately 45 minutes for most walkers. Trekking poles are very useful here. The trail drops from around 200 m at the clifftop to beach level at Tunkalilla — more than 190 m of descent in under 3 km.

Tide Check — Before Reaching Tunkalilla

Check the Bureau of Meteorology Victor Harbor tide chart before setting out. Aim to arrive at Tunkalilla Beach within 2 hours either side of low tide. At high tide, waves can close off the beach entirely by running directly to the cliff base — leaving no passable route and no safe retreat. This is not an exaggeration.

Tunkalilla Beach (km 5.5 – 7)

Tunkalilla Beach is one of the most remote and beautiful beaches in South Australia. Dark sand, sheer cliffs on both sides, wave-scoured rock platforms and the constant thunder of Southern Ocean swell — and, in all likelihood, not another person in sight.

There is no infrastructure, no safe swimming and no shelter. The 1.5 km beach crossing is straightforward in normal conditions: walk south along the sand until the cliffs converge, then pick up the track ascending the eastern headland.

Whale Watching from Tunkalilla

Between June and October, Tunkalilla Beach offers some of the most raw, unmediated whale watching in the state. Southern right whales pass close inshore along this stretch, and with no infrastructure, no crowds and 200 m cliffs behind you, it's an experience that's hard to overstate. Even outside whale season, orca are occasionally sighted offshore.

Eastern Headland Scramble (km 7 – 9.5)

The eastern headland scramble is the crux of the walk. The trail climbs steeply from beach level over the headland — with sections of rock scrambling where trekking poles need to be collapsed and hands used for balance. It’s not technically climbing, but it’s the most demanding single section.

Allow more time than you think. The views from the top of the headland back toward Tunkalilla Beach — and west toward Tapanappa — are the best on the walk.

Coastal Heath to Balquhidder (km 9.5 – 14.75)

From the headland, the trail rolls through coastal heath and open clifftop country heading east toward Balquhidder. The terrain eases considerably after the headland scramble — this final section is the most enjoyable and relaxed part of the walk.

The trail emerges at Balquhidder campsite — a designated Heysen Trail camp with a basic shelter, a composting toilet and a water tank (do not rely on the tank being filled — verify in advance with the Heysen Trail Association).

Getting There and Away

Start: Tapanappa Lookout, accessible via Delamere Road from the Fleurieu Peninsula. The road to Tapanappa is unsealed for the final section — accessible in a normal vehicle in dry conditions, but 4WD advisable after heavy rain.

Finish: Balquhidder campsite. By road, Balquhidder is approximately 1.5 hours from Tapanappa via Delamere, Yankalilla and Carrickalinga. Plan your shuttle carefully before setting out.

Who Is This Walk For?

Experienced walkers who are:

  • Fit enough for 15 km with 600 m ascent in remote terrain
  • Comfortable with tide-dependent planning
  • Equipped with navigation tools and a PLB or satellite device
  • Willing to accept the commitment level involved

This is not a walk for the unprepared. For fit, experienced walkers who embrace remote coastal adventure, it’s exceptional.

Practical Information

Tips for Walkers

One of the wildest day sections on the Heysen Trail South Coast. This is remote terrain — go fully prepared.

Water — Carry Everything

There is absolutely no water on this route. The only water at Tunkalilla Beach is salt water. Carry a minimum of 3 litres from the start. Balquhidder campsite has a tank but it cannot be relied upon — verify current status with the Heysen Trail Association before departure.

Shuttle — Essential

Tapanappa Lookout and Balquhidder campsite are only connected by foot on the Heysen Trail — no road links the two trailheads. By road from Balquhidder to Tapanappa is approximately 1.5 hours via Delamere. Plan your shuttle carefully.

Remote and Committing

Once you descend to Tunkalilla Beach, the only exits are back up the cliffs you came down or forward over the headland. Mobile phone coverage is absent for most of this route. Share your trip plan with someone before departing.

Navigation Critical

This section follows cliff edges, beach rocks and headland scrambles where the trail is intermittently marked. Download the GPX and have it on your phone AND a dedicated GPS device. The cliff edges in morning fog have caused walker incidents — do not proceed in poor visibility.

Footwear — No Compromise

Waterproof hiking boots with ankle support and a firm sole are not optional on this section. Rocky coastal scrambles, loose cliff paths and wet beach rock require proper grip. Trail runners will leave you struggling.

Tidal Awareness — Tunkalilla Beach

The Tunkalilla Beach section is tide-dependent. At high tide the beach may be fully closed by waves against the cliffs. Check tide times before departure and plan to cross Tunkalilla at low or mid tide. The Bureau of Meteorology's Victor Harbor tide chart applies.

Cliff Edges — Real Hazard

The clifftop sections between Tapanappa and Boat Harbour have unfenced drops of 50–120 m directly to the Southern Ocean. Wind gusts are strong and unpredictable. Stay well back from cliff edges, particularly in gusty conditions. Children require close supervision.

Camping at Balquhidder

Balquhidder is a designated Heysen Trail campsite with a basic shelter and water tank. Camping requires a permit from the Heysen Trail Association. The campsite is also a logical end point for walkers doing the multi-day South Coast section.
