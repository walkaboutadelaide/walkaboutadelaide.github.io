Happy Valley Reservoir Walks — Complete Guide

Everything you need to know about walking the Happy Valley Reservoir Reserve, plus every loop trail reviewed.

4 walks Happy Valley adelaide hills reservoir loop

Happy Valley Reservoir is a working drinking-water reservoir in Adelaide’s southern suburbs, set inside a large reserve of open woodland, native grassland and reservoir foreshore. It is one of several South Australian reservoir reserves opened to the public for low-impact recreation — and it has quickly become one of the best loop-walking destinations close to the city.

This page is the single home for everything that applies to all of the walks here — access, facilities, rules and the lay of the land — so the individual trail reviews can stay focused on the walking itself. Read this once, then pick a loop below.

Where It Is & Getting There

The reserve sits in Happy Valley, roughly 20 km south of the Adelaide CBD — about a 25–30 minute drive. The main trailhead and car park are off Chandlers Hill Road. There is sealed parking, and the reserve is signposted from the main roads.

Public transport does not run directly to the trailheads, so a car is the practical option for most visitors.

Opening Hours & Access

As a managed SA Water reserve, Happy Valley Reservoir has gated opening hours that change with the season (typically opening early morning and closing late afternoon/evening, earlier in winter). Entry is free.

Always check the current opening times with SA Water before you go — the gates are locked outside hours and seasonal changes are common. Plan to be back at the car park well before closing time, especially on the longer Boundary Loop.

Facilities

The main trailhead area is well set up for a half-day visit:

  • Sealed car park
  • Public toilets
  • Picnic areas and public BBQs
  • Drinking water
  • Shade and seating near the foreshore

There are no facilities out on the trails themselves, so fill water bottles and use the toilets before you set off — particularly for the longer loops.

Rules & What to Know

Because it protects a drinking-water supply, the reserve has a few firm rules that apply on every walk:

  • No dogs anywhere in the reserve
  • No swimming or water contact in the reservoir
  • No cycling on the walking trails unless signed as shared-use
  • Stay on the formed trails — the surrounding land is a protected catchment
  • Take all rubbish with you

These rules are consistent across all the loops, which is why you will not see them repeated on each trail review.

The Trail Network

The walks here are a connected family of loops radiating from the same trailhead, so you can mix and match by fitness and time:

  • Short & flat — a quick woodland circuit, ideal for families, prams and a lunch-break walk.
  • Medium loops — foreshore and woodland combinations with gentle-to-moderate hills.
  • The full Boundary Loop — the complete perimeter circuit with steep pinches and a link toward Glenthorne National Park for anyone wanting a longer day.

Surfaces are mostly formed gravel and compacted dirt. The terrain is deceptively hilly in places — the longer loops have short, sharp climbs that add up.

Best Time to Visit

March to October is ideal — mild temperatures and green countryside. The reserve is exposed in summer with limited shade on the open sections, so in the warmer months go early and carry plenty of water. Mornings are best for birdlife on the foreshore.

Conditions, gate times and facility availability can change. Treat this guide as a starting point and confirm current details with SA Water before heading out.
